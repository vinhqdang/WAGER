### Summary Assessment *
The manuscript decomposes the paired proper-score difference between two frozen probabilistic classifiers, conditional on a declared discrete grouping variable $\phi$, into a part that survives within-group relabelling ($\Delta P$) and a remainder ($\Delta R$) that is an order-two U-statistic equal to a within-cell covariance between the models' forecast contrast and the label indicator. The identity is exact in finite samples, the proofs (Appendix A) are elementary and correct, and the estimator comes with a Hájek influence function, a cluster-robust sandwich variance, a CLT, and a within-cell randomization test. The applied audit of two released scene-graph checkpoints is a real and useful piece of work.

The framing, however, is wrong on its central point. $\Delta R$ is **not** the resolution term of the Murphy/DeGroot–Fienberg/Bröcker partition. It is a Yates-type forecast–outcome covariance, which equals *twice* the resolution difference only when both forecasts are calibrated within cells; in general it carries an additional reliability×resolution cross term. The manuscript's own §6 observation that $\Delta R$ is not invariant to monotone recalibration is a proof of this, since the classical resolution term depends on the forecast only through the partition it generates and is therefore recalibration-invariant. A cluster of related literature-accuracy errors sits in the same subsection. The concession about point estimates combining, which I was asked to check specifically, is now **correct**.

The strongest finding is a Critical positioning error; the residual novelty, once corrected, is short-communication sized. Both are repairable, hence Major Revision.

## Strengths *

### S1: The corrected concession about combining point estimates is now right, and precisely right
§3.1 concedes that the two models' separately estimated in-sample quantities *do* combine, and gives the correct reason: both models are scored on the same $\phi$-partition, hence the same per-cell $n_c$, hence the same $(n_c-1)/n_c$ factor. I verified this independently. The whole estimator is linear in the gain vector $H_i(\cdot)$, and $H=S(q_1,\cdot)-S(q_0,\cdot)$, so $\widehat{\Delta R}=\widehat R^{(1)}-\widehat R^{(0)}$ term for term, and the in-sample plug-in of each single-model quantity carries the identical multiplicative factor. Subtracting two naive plug-ins therefore does recover exactly the biased contrast estimate, as claimed. I was told an earlier version stated the opposite; the current statement is sound and its "term for term" is literally true, not rhetorical.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.1 "both models share the same $\phi$-partition and hence the same per-cell $n_c$, so the same $(n_c-1)/n_c$ attenuation"`

### S2: The novelty claim in §3.1 itself is honestly bounded
The subsection explicitly disowns the two overclaims a reader of this literature would suspect: that a new scoring rule is being proposed, and that $\Delta R$ is a new quantity. Very few papers that touch this partition are this direct about building on it. My criticism below is that the abstract, title, contribution list and highlights do not honour the restraint §3.1 shows.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.1 "WAGER is not a new scoring rule, and $\Delta R$ is not a new quantity to be evaluated on its own"`

### S3: The coarsening identity is a genuinely useful contribution to how a conditioning set is chosen
Proposition 2 is a per-label law of total covariance summed over $y$, so it is elementary, but stating it as the exact mechanism by which a coarser conditioning variable changes the credited remainder — with a non-sign-definite between-cell covariance as the discrepancy — is the right formalisation of a question this literature usually handles informally (Bröcker's sufficiency ordering treats refinement qualitatively). This is the one place where I think the paper adds something a verification statistician would want to cite.
**Evidence Anchor**: `equation: Proposition 2 (coarsening decomposition) — the between-cell covariance term $\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbb E[H_X(y)\mid C],\Pr(Y=y\mid C)\mid\bar\phi=\bar c)$`

### S4: Scope limits are stated unusually plainly, including the one that undermines the paper's own framing
§6 states that $\Delta R$ is not invariant to monotone recalibration, that unrecorded within-cell shortcuts are credited to $\Delta R$, and that a prior-fit gain is not illegitimate. The first of these is, as I argue in W1, the disproof of the paper's headline identification — the author has the decisive fact in hand and has not seen what it implies. Honest reporting of it is nonetheless to the author's credit and makes the repair straightforward.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §6 "$\Delta R$ is not invariant to monotone recalibration"`

### S5: Correct, checkable, elementary proofs
Appendix A's proofs of Theorem 1, Theorem 3, Theorem 2 (finite-sample identity), Proposition 2 and Proposition 4 are short and I verified each by hand. The Bregman argument's key step — the $y$-independent part of $H_x(y)$ drops out because $\sum_y\ind\{Y=y\}=1$ a.s. — is correct, and the paper is right that properness is not used for the identity itself.

## Weaknesses *

### W1: $\Delta R$ is not the classical resolution term; the identification is asserted as exact and is false as stated
**Problem**: The paper's central positioning claim is that $\Delta R$ is the resolution term of the classical reliability–resolution decomposition applied to a pair rather than one forecaster, and it is asserted as an exact identification ("in exactly this sense") in §3.1, in the abstract, in the intro, in Table 1, in §4.10 and in the conclusion. It is not exact. Work it out for a single model in one cell, with the paper's own quadratic score $S_{\rm B}(q,y)=2q_y-\lVert q\rVert_2^2 = 1-\mathrm{BS}(q,y)$, and let $\pi_y=\Pr(Y=y\mid q,c)$ be the calibrated forecast, so that the Murphy partition inside the cell reads $\mathrm{BS}_c=\mathrm{REL}_c-\mathrm{RES}_c+\mathrm{UNC}_c$ with $\mathrm{REL}_c=\mathbb E[\sum_y(q_y-\pi_y)^2\mid c]$ and $\mathrm{RES}_c=\mathbb E[\sum_y(\pi_y-p_y(c))^2\mid c]$. The transported score is the score of $q$ against the cell base rate, $\mathbb E[\sum_y(q_y-p_y(c))^2\mid c]+\mathrm{UNC}_c$, and expanding through $\pi$ gives
$$R_c \;=\; 2\,\mathrm{RES}_c \;+\; 2\,\mathrm{X}_c,\qquad \mathrm{X}_c=\sum_y\mathbb E\big[(q_y-\pi_y)(\pi_y-p_y(c))\,\big|\,c\big].$$
Hence for the pair, $\Delta R_c = 2(\mathrm{RES}^{(1)}_c-\mathrm{RES}^{(0)}_c)+2(\mathrm{X}^{(1)}_c-\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_c)$. Two things are wrong with the paper's statement: a factor of two on the same score scale, and an omitted reliability×resolution cross term that vanishes only under within-cell calibration. Both are easy to check numerically. Take a two-point cell with base rate $(1/2,1/2)$, an uninformative-but-calibrated $q_0=(1/2,1/2)$ and a perfect $q_1$: the paper reports $\Delta T=0.5=\Delta P+\Delta R=-0.5+1.0$, while $\Delta\mathrm{RES}=0.5$. And for the cross term, a binary forecaster taking values $\{0.2,0.8\}$ with $\pi\in\{0.4,0.6\}$ has $\sum_y\mathrm{X}=0.04\neq 0$.

There is a second, independent and completely decisive argument, and it is in the paper already. The classical resolution term depends on the forecast only through the $\sigma$-algebra (partition) the forecast generates — that is exactly what Bröcker (2009), cited here, makes explicit — so it is *invariant* under any strictly monotone recalibration of a fixed forecast. §6 correctly reports that $\Delta R$ is *not* invariant to monotone recalibration, and Appendix E quantifies the effect on real models. A quantity that moves under a transformation that leaves the classical resolution term fixed cannot be the classical resolution term.

**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.1 "Our $\Delta R$ is a resolution \emph{difference} in exactly this sense, and we build on the fact rather than obscure it"`
**Why it matters**: This is the manuscript's entire answer to the previous referee's complaint that the contributions were not stated in simple, precise statistical terms. The answer given is precise and wrong, which is worse than vague. It propagates into the title, the abstract's closing sentence, contribution 1, Table 1, §4.10 and the conclusion, and into the cover letter. A reader in this literature will check it in ten minutes and stop reading.
**Suggestion**: State the true relation, which is a better result than the one claimed because it is correct and it explains the paper's own calibration-matching control. Specifically: (i) $\Delta R$ is the *Yates-type covariance* term — twice the within-cell covariance between the models' forecast contrast and the label indicator; (ii) under within-cell calibration of both forecasts it equals $2(\mathrm{RES}^{(1)}-\mathrm{RES}^{(0)})$, which is precisely why calibration matching is required rather than merely advisable; (iii) in general the discrepancy is the reliability×resolution cross term $2(\mathrm{X}^{(1)}-\mathrm{X}^{(0)})$, whose sign is not determined. Add this as a short proposition; it is two lines given Theorem 1, it converts §6's caveat into a theorem, and it retires the recalibration objection instead of conceding it. Then delete "in exactly this sense", "which identifies it as", and the corresponding claims in the title/abstract/highlights.
**Severity**: Critical
**Confidence**: 5 — core expertise: proper-score decompositions; the algebra above and both numerical checks were done by hand, and the recalibration-invariance argument uses only the paper's own §6 statement plus the standard definition of resolution.

### W2: The classical resolution term is misdescribed as a forecast–outcome covariance, and that representation is misattributed
**Problem**: §3.1 defines the classical resolution term as "a covariance between the forecast and the outcome indicator within groups" and attributes it to Murphy (1973) and DeGroot & Fienberg (1983). Neither paper's resolution term is that covariance. Murphy's vector partition gives resolution as a variance of conditional outcome frequencies, $\sum_k (n_k/n)\sum_y(\bar o_{ky}-\bar o_y)^2$; DeGroot & Fienberg's refinement/sufficiency framing likewise works with the conditional label distributions, not with a raw forecast–outcome covariance. The correct covariance statement is that resolution equals the covariance between the *recalibrated* forecast $\pi=\mathbb E[Y\mid q]$ and the outcome, and $\operatorname{Cov}(q,Y)=\operatorname{Var}(\pi)+\operatorname{Cov}(q-\pi,\pi)$ differs from it by exactly the cross term of W1. The covariance *representation* of the Brier score is Yates's (1982) decomposition, and the manuscript reaches it only through a 2026 arXiv rearrangement of Yates, cited as a mere "alternative covariance rearrangement". Yates (1982) itself is absent from `ref.bib`. This is a textbook secondhand citation: the paper's own $\Delta R$ is a Yates covariance term, and Yates is the one antecedent not cited.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.1 "the latter a covariance between the forecast and the outcome indicator within groups"`
**Why it matters**: The whole force of "in exactly this sense" in the next paragraph rests on this definitional sentence. Because the definition is wrong, the identification it licenses is wrong; and because the true antecedent (Yates) is cited only at second hand, the paper looks as though it has not read the literature it is positioning against. Both problems live in one sentence.
**Suggestion**: Cite Yates (1982) directly and state that $\Delta R$ is a Yates-type covariance contrast. Keep Murphy/DeGroot–Fienberg/Bröcker for the reliability–resolution–uncertainty partition, described correctly (see W4). Keep the 2026 rearrangement as a recent restatement, not as the source.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 5 — core expertise: I can attest Murphy's and Yates's forms; see Missing Key References for the metadata I am and am not sure of.

### W3: "Prior-fit gain" is a misnomer: $\Delta P$ rewards reduced sharpness independently of any improvement in fitting group label frequencies
**Problem**: $\Delta P$ is the gain in score when each model's forecast is scored against an independently drawn within-cell label. A sharper forecast always scores worse against a randomly drawn label, whether or not it fits the cell's label frequencies any better. So $\Delta P$ conflates two distinct things: better fit to the group's label histogram, and lower sharpness. A counterexample in the paper's own four-point cell (label frequencies $(3/4,1/4)$): let the old model be perfectly resolving, $q_0=(1,0)$ on the three $y=1$ cases and $(0,1)$ on the $y=2$ case, and let the new model predict the base rate $(3/4,1/4)$ everywhere. Both models' *average* forecast in the cell is exactly the base rate, so neither fits the cell's label frequencies better than the other. Yet the decomposition returns $\Delta T=-0.375$, $\Delta R=-0.75$, and $\Delta P=+0.375$: a large positive "prior-fit gain" purely from desharpening. Relatedly, for two within-cell-calibrated models the identity forces $\Delta P=-\Delta\mathrm{RES}$ and $\Delta R=+2\Delta\mathrm{RES}$, so a negative "prior-fit gain" is an automatic algebraic shadow of a positive resolution gain rather than evidence that prior fit deteriorated. Appendix E repeatedly reads negative prior terms as substantive prior-side deficits.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §1.3 Table 1, row $\Delta P$ — "the part of $\Delta T$ that survives label transport", standard counterpart given as "---"`
**Why it matters**: The flagship empirical claim is that a ten-point mean-recall improvement is "overwhelmingly prior-fit". With $\Delta P$ as defined, that sentence does not distinguish "TDE fits the class-pair label frequencies better" from "TDE is less sharp within class-pair cells". Temperature matching equalises global confidence but does not equalise within-cell sharpness, so the control does not close the gap. Note that the *audit's* substantive conclusion survives intact — $\Delta R\approx 0$ still says there is no within-cell resolution gain — but the attribution of the remainder to "group-frequency fit" does not.
**Suggestion**: Rename $\Delta P$ to something that describes what it is (e.g. "transported gain", or "gain against the within-cell base rate") and state its decomposition explicitly: for a contrast, $\Delta P=-(\Delta\mathrm{REL}+\Delta\mathrm{RES}+2\Delta\mathrm{X})$, with the uncertainty term cancelling. Then reword every claim of the form "the gain is overwhelmingly prior-fit" as a claim about what is *not* in $\Delta R$. Table 1's "---" for $\Delta P$'s standard counterpart is also internally inconsistent with W1's claim: if $\Delta R$ really were the resolution difference, $\Delta P$ would necessarily be the reliability difference, which does have a standard counterpart.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 5 — core expertise: sharpness/calibration decomposition; the counterexample was computed by hand and is reproducible in four lines of arithmetic.

### W4: The three-term partition is described throughout as two-term, and the uncertainty term is never mentioned
**Problem**: The abstract, §1, §3.1, Table 1, §4.10, the conclusion and the cover letter all call the classical result the "reliability–resolution decomposition". Murphy's (1973) vector partition — whose title, "A New Vector Partition of the Probability Score", the paper's own bibliography records — is $\mathrm{REL}-\mathrm{RES}+\mathrm{UNC}$, and Bröcker (2009) generalises all three. The word "uncertainty" appears nowhere in the manuscript in this sense.
**Evidence Anchor**: `absence: §3.1 and every framing passage — expected acknowledgement of the uncertainty term and a statement that it cancels in a pairwise contrast; checked abstract, §1.2, §1.3 Table 1, §3.1, §4.2, §4.10, §6, §7, Appendix A`
**Why it matters**: For a paper whose entire framing is this partition, describing it in two terms reads as unfamiliarity, and it conceals exactly the point that helps the author: $\mathrm{UNC}$ cancels in a contrast, which is a genuine advantage of targeting the pair and is currently unclaimed. It also hides where the uncertainty-scale content sits in $\Delta P$ (see W3).
**Suggestion**: Call it the reliability–resolution–uncertainty partition, and add one sentence noting that $\mathrm{UNC}$ depends on the labels alone and therefore cancels in the paired contrast — a small positive point for the paper, currently forfeited.
**Severity**: Minor
**Confidence**: 5 — core expertise: the partition's canonical form.

### W5: The multiclass and general-strictly-proper extensions are credited to a 2026 preprint that the paper's own earlier citations already cover
**Problem**: §3.1 says the partition "continues to be extended — to general strictly proper and multiclass scores" and cites Charpentier & Fernandes Machado (2026, arXiv). Both extensions predate that preprint by decades and are cited two sentences earlier in the same paragraph: the multicategory (vector) case *is* Murphy (1973), and the extension to general proper scores via generalized entropy and sufficiency *is* Bröcker (2009), with the surrounding framework in Gneiting & Raftery (2007), which the paper also cites.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.1 "The partition continues to be extended --- to general strictly proper and multiclass scores"`
**Why it matters**: This is a factual error about prior work in the subsection that carries the paper's positioning, and it is self-refuting against the paper's own reference list. An editor who spots it will discount the rest of the literature section.
**Suggestion**: Attribute the multicategory case to Murphy (1973) and the general-proper-score case to Bröcker (2009); describe the 2026 preprint as whatever it actually adds beyond them.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 5 — core expertise: I can attest Murphy (1973) is the vector/multicategory partition and Bröcker (2009) the general-proper-score generalisation. I have not read the 2026 preprint and make no claim about its content, only about the priority of the two extensions named.

### W6: The equation of Proposition 4's $(n_c-1)/n_c$ attenuation with the bias Ferro & Fricker remove is asserted without proof and appears to be wrong in form and sign
**Problem**: §3.1 identifies the $(n_c-1)/n_c$ shrinkage of the in-sample plug-in with "the finite-sample bias a bias-corrected single-model decomposition already removes for one forecaster", citing Ferro & Fricker (2012). These are different biases. Proposition 4's factor is the familiar $1/n$-versus-$1/(n-1)$ attenuation of a plug-in *covariance* — a multiplicative, downward bias, which is what one gets because the paper's estimand is linear in $H$ with the estimated cell frequencies entering multiplicatively. The plug-in resolution term of the Murphy partition is quadratic in the estimated within-bin frequencies and is biased *upward* by an additive term of order $\sum_k w_k\sum_y p_{ky}(1-p_{ky})/n_k$; Ferro & Fricker's corrections (and Stephenson, Coelho & Jolliffe's extra components, and Bröcker 2012) are additive corrections of that form. An additive upward correction is not a multiplicative downward shrinkage.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.1 "the finite-sample bias a bias-corrected single-model decomposition already removes for one forecaster"`
**Why it matters**: This clause is load-bearing: it is the sentence that makes Proposition 4 look like a known result already handled for a single model, which is what licenses the paper to concede the point estimates and claim only the inference. If the two biases are not the same, the author has understated a real advantage (the leave-one-out U-statistic is *exactly* unbiased at the full sample, whereas the classical route needs an approximate additive correction) while asserting something inaccurate about Ferro & Fricker. The claim as written also depends on the covariance representation the paper wrongly attributes to Murphy (W2), so the two errors are linked.
**Suggestion**: Either prove the equivalence explicitly, in the covariance representation and with Ferro & Fricker's correction written out, or replace the clause with the stronger and correct statement: in the covariance representation the finite-sample bias is exactly multiplicative and the leave-one-out U-statistic removes it exactly, whereas the classical partition's plug-in requires an approximate additive correction. Cite Stephenson–Coelho–Jolliffe (2008) and Bröcker (2012) alongside Ferro & Fricker (2012).
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 — core expertise: finite-sample bias in the empirical Brier decomposition. I am confident about the sign and functional form of the plug-in resolution bias and about the additive form of the published corrections; I have not re-derived Ferro & Fricker's exact expressions in this session, so I frame this as a claim the author must substantiate rather than a proven contradiction.

### W7: The "no covariance is available" gap is overstated, because the literature on inference for skill *differences* between two forecast systems is missing
**Problem**: The characterisation of Siegert (2014) is fair — it is a single-system variance estimator and it does not supply a cross-model covariance. But the paper converts that into a gap in the field, and the field is not silent. Forecast verification has a developed literature on testing skill differences between two systems evaluated on shared verification data (Ferro 2007 on comparing probabilistic systems with the Brier score; DelSole & Tippett 2014 on comparing forecast skill; Siegert et al. 2017 on detecting improvements in correlation skill). None is cited; the entire comparative-verification literature is represented by Diebold–Mariano and Giacomini–White from econometrics. Separately, the "rebuild from the joint law" that §3.1 describes as the alternative is a routine bivariate delta-method / joint-influence-function exercise, and the paper's own citation of DeLong, DeLong & Clarke-Pearson (1988) is an example of it being done for two correlated statistics on one sample. Presenting it as the burden the paper spares the reader overstates the difficulty.
**Evidence Anchor**: `absence: §3.1–§3.2 — expected engagement with inference for Brier-score or skill-score differences between two forecast systems on shared verification data; checked §3.1, §3.2, §3.3, §3.4 and ref.bib (all 63 entries)`
**Why it matters**: The size of the gap is the paper's novelty argument. A referee who knows this literature reads the argument as "no one has computed this particular covariance", which is a much smaller claim than the text implies, and the missing citations make the omission look like unfamiliarity rather than a scoping decision.
**Suggestion**: Cite the skill-difference literature and say precisely what remains: that no published estimator targets the *resolution-side* contrast for a pair on a declared conditioning partition with a one-pass cluster-robust influence function. That claim I believe survives, and it is worth making narrowly.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 — core expertise: comparative forecast verification. High confidence that this literature exists and is relevant; moderate confidence on exact metadata for individual items (see Missing Key References).

### W8: A specific false sentence in §4.10: a resolution term "applied without any conditioning at all" is identically zero
**Problem**: The Corollary 1 discussion states that at the trivial one-cell partition $\widehat{\Delta R}$ "is then exactly the classical resolution term of §3.1's single-model decomposition, applied without any conditioning at all, and $\widehat{\Delta P}$ the corresponding calibration term". Resolution is defined relative to a conditioning set; with the trivial conditioning set it is identically zero. What the sentence presumably means is "with no conditioning beyond the forecast itself" — but that is precisely the classical setting, not a degenerate one, and the paper's $\Delta R$ at the trivial $\phi$ is still not that term (W1). The claim about $\widehat{\Delta P}$ is also wrong: at the trivial partition $\widehat{\Delta P}$ is the score against the global label marginal, i.e. reliability plus resolution plus the cross term (with uncertainty), not the calibration term.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §4.10 "applied without any conditioning at all, and $\widehat{\Delta P}$ the corresponding calibration term"`
**Why it matters**: This sentence is where a domain reader will test the positioning claim, because the trivial partition is the one case where the mapping should be transparent. It fails there, in a way that exposes an unresolved confusion between the two roles conditioning plays in this construction: $\phi$ stratifies *before* the classical decomposition, whereas the classical conditioning variable is the forecast. Making that distinction explicit would improve the paper independently of W1.
**Suggestion**: Rewrite the passage to distinguish the two conditioning layers, and state the trivial-partition case in the corrected form of W1: at $\phi\equiv$ const, $\Delta R$ is twice the unconditional Yates covariance contrast, equal to twice the unconditional resolution difference under calibration.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 5 — core expertise: definition of resolution relative to a conditioning $\sigma$-algebra.

### W9: The abstract drops the factor of two that Theorem 1 carries
**Problem**: The abstract says the remainder is "equal to the within-group covariance between the two models' predicted-probability difference and the label indicator". Theorem 1's quadratic-score identity is $\Delta R_c=2\sum_y\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta q_y,\ind\{Y=y\}\mid c)$. The general Bregman statement (Theorem 3) has no factor of two because it uses the gradient contrast $\Delta g_y$, and for the quadratic score $\Delta g_y=2\Delta q_y$; the abstract mixes the two by using the probability difference with the gradient-form coefficient.
**Evidence Anchor**: `equation: Theorem 1, Eq. for $\Delta R_c$ under the quadratic score — the coefficient 2 on $\sum_y\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta q_y,\ind\{Y=y\}\mid c)$`
**Why it matters**: The abstract is where the identification claim is made most compactly, and the factor of two is exactly one of the two discrepancies in W1. Getting it wrong here makes the false identification look tidier than it is.
**Suggestion**: Say "twice the within-group covariance" for the quadratic score, or state the abstract's claim in the Bregman gradient form.
**Severity**: Minor
**Confidence**: 5 — core expertise; verified against the paper's own equation.

If the corrected relation of W1 is stated, "resolution" can stay in the body with its caveat.
**Severity**: Minor
**Confidence**: 4 — core expertise on the statistical term; the imaging-sense concern is an adjacent-field judgement about how these venues read the word.

### W11: The abstract's headline empirical sentence does not survive compression
**Problem**: The abstract closes "a ten-point gain in a widely reported metric is overwhelmingly group-frequency fit". ## Detailed Comments *

### Title & Abstract
The title asserts the identification that W1 shows is inexact, and repeats the term collision of W10. The abstract compresses two claims past the point of accuracy (W9, W11). Everything else in the abstract is an accurate summary of the paper.

### Introduction
§1's motivating example (class-pair priors in relation prediction) is well chosen and the one-sentence statement of the construction is genuinely clear — this part of the rewrite worked. §1.2's paragraph "What the second component is, in standard terms" is where the Critical error enters the paper, and Table 1 in §1.3 is where it is hardened into a lookup table. §1.4's scope paragraph is honest and well judged.

### Literature Review / Theoretical Framework
- **Coverage**: The reordering to lead with the statistics literature is the right decision and the section now reads as a statistics paper's related work. Coverage of the *decomposition* strand is thin in a specific way: it goes Murphy → DeGroot–Fienberg → Bröcker → three 2025/2026 preprints, skipping the entire 1982–2021 middle, which is where the covariance representation (Yates), the finite-sample corrections (Stephenson et al.; Bröcker; Ferro & Fricker; Siegert) and the modern nonparametric treatment (Dimitriadis, Gneiting & Jordan) live. Two of those items are cited but only in the narrow bias/variance role in the second paragraph. The comparative-verification strand is missing entirely (W7). The applied strand (§3.4) is dense and, so far as I can judge, appropriate; it is not my seat.
- **Integration quality**: Genuinely critical synthesis rather than enumeration — each paragraph ends by naming what is and is not taken from the cited work, and §3.3's "the new element is the gain-contrast transport identity ... not permutation or U-statistic theory by itself" is exactly the right register. The problem is not integration but accuracy of the claims being integrated (W2, W5, W6).
- **Research gap argument**: Structurally sound but overstated in size (W7). Narrowed as I suggest, it is defensible.
- **Theoretical framework — appropriateness**: The reliability–resolution–uncertainty partition is the right framework for this object, and the Bregman-score family is the right level of generality for the covariance form. No better-fitting alternative framework exists; I considered the calibration/refinement (DeGroot–Fienberg) route and the CORP/isotonic route and neither is preferable for a pairwise contrast.
- **Application depth**: This is where the paper is weakest in my remit. The framework is *named* accurately in its citations but never *applied*: nowhere does the paper write down the map between $(\Delta T,\Delta P,\Delta R)$ and $(\Delta\mathrm{REL},\Delta\mathrm{RES},\Delta\mathrm{UNC})$. Had that half-page been written, W1, W3, W4 and W8 would all have been caught by the author. I recommend adding it as a subsection; it is the single highest-value revision available.

### Contribution to the Field
- **Incremental contribution, stated plainly**: Once the resolution connection is granted (in its corrected form), what remains genuinely new is: (a) an exactly unbiased, nuisance-free leave-one-out U-statistic for the within-cell forecast–label covariance *contrast*, together with the exact algebraic identity $\widehat{\Delta T}=\widehat{\Delta P}+\widehat{\Delta R}$ — no fitted reference forecaster, no sample splitting, no smoothing rule, and no approximate additive bias correction; (b) a one-pass cluster-robust influence function and CLT for that contrast; (c) the exact coarsening identity (Prop. 2) as a tool for reasoning about the choice of conditioning variable. That is a real contribution and I would not dismiss it. It is also a *short-communication-sized* contribution to this literature: (a) is the $1/n$-versus-$1/(n-1)$ correction in a covariance representation, (b) is a standard Hájek projection plus Lindeberg–Feller, and (c) is the law of total covariance. Theorem 3 (Bregman) adds little, since the paper itself proves the identity holds for *any* score and the Bregman restriction only supplies the interpretation of $\Delta g_y$; Proposition 3's data-free bound is admitted to be three orders of magnitude loose; Corollary 1 is stated as immediate from the definitions.
- **Does the paper claim more or less than that?** §3.1 claims almost exactly the right amount and even disclaims more. The front matter claims more: the title asserts the identification, the abstract closes on it, contribution 1 is titled "An exact decomposition, and what its remainder is", contribution 3 presents three elementary lemmas as "two exact statements about design choices that would otherwise be guesswork", and the highlights assert "The remainder is the classical resolution term". So the paper is internally inconsistent in claim size, with the honest version buried in §3.1 and the inflated version in every high-visibility location. Aligning the front matter *down* to §3.1's level, and correcting §3.1 per W1–W2, is the revision I would ask for.
- **Positioning**: Explicitly and, apart from the errors above, carefully done. No plagiarism-adjacent or priority-grabbing behaviour; the paper repeatedly says what it is not.
- **Overclaiming risk**: Concentrated entirely in the resolution identification (W1) and in the naming of $\Delta P$ (W3). The empirical claims are, if anything, under-claimed relative to the evidence.

### Did the rewrite introduce inaccuracy?
Yes, in three ways, all traceable to the mechanical substitution. (i) The substitution created the false identification by *naming*: once the second component is called "resolution" throughout, sentences like §3.1's "in exactly this sense" and §4.10's "exactly the classical resolution term" follow rhetorically rather than mathematically, and no proof was added to support them (W1, W8). (ii) It created a collision with the classical term's established meaning in the title, keywords and Table 1, so the paper now asserts by vocabulary what it has not shown (W10). The imaging sense of "resolution" is a live secondary risk given the applications. (iii) It left agreement artifacts at three places where the replaced word began with a vowel — see Minor Issues. I found no case where "resolution" was substituted into a sentence that became mathematically false *purely* by the substitution and independently of W1; the damage is the framing, not local sentence corruption. The residual label `eq:sample-reason` and the surviving "alignment"/"aligns" phrasings are harmless traces.

## Questions for Authors *

1. Please write out the map between $(\Delta T,\Delta P,\Delta R)$ and the reliability–resolution–uncertainty partition of the same score on the same $\phi$-partition, and state the conditions under which $\Delta R$ equals a multiple of the resolution difference. My own calculation gives $\Delta R_c=2(\mathrm{RES}^{(1)}_c-\mathrm{RES}^{(0)}_c)+2(\mathrm{X}^{(1)}_c-\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_c)$ with $\mathrm{X}_c=\sum_y\mathbb E[(q_y-\pi_y)(\pi_y-p_y(c))\mid c]$; if you disagree, where?
2. Since the classical resolution term is invariant under strictly monotone recalibration of a fixed forecast (it depends on the forecast only through the partition it generates), and §6 reports that $\Delta R$ is not, how do you reconcile the identification claimed in §3.1, the abstract and the title with your own §6?
