### Confidence Score
5 — U-statistics, influence-function inference, cluster-robust variance estimation and randomization tests are my core areas, and I was able to re-derive and independently recompute essentially every formal claim in the paper.

### Summary Assessment
The paper gives an exact finite-sample decomposition of a paired proper-score difference into a component recoverable from within-group label frequencies and a within-group resolution remainder, the latter an order-two U-statistic with an antisymmetric kernel; it supplies a Bregman-family generalization, a coarsening identity, a Cauchy–Schwarz sensitivity bound, an exact attenuation result for the in-sample plug-in, cluster-robust inference, and an audit of two released scene-graph checkpoints. The algebraic core is correct and unusually well verified: Theorems 1–3, Corollary 1 and Propositions 1 and 3 all reproduce exactly under my own independent recomputation, the new Section 2 worked example is right to the last eighth, and all 83 quoted numbers trace to committed results.

The weaknesses are concentrated in the newest layer — Theorem 4 and Proposition 2 — and in how the estimand is reported. Theorem 4's printed sandwich-variance formula is wrong by a factor of *G* (the appendix and the code are right), its conditions (iii) and (v) cannot hold simultaneously in the regime the paper advertises, and the step from a per-cell Hájek remainder to the dataset-level `o_p(N_*^{-1/2})` is asserted rather than derived. The paper's honesty statement about condition (v) rests on a factual claim about its own data ("most eligible VG150 cells sit at the minimum size n_c=2") that is false and refutable from the paper's own Table E.7. Corollary 2's worst-case bound throws away the simplex constraint and is 3.6× looser than it needs to be, which also misdiagnoses the looseness the paper spends a main-text table lamenting. Finally, the identification of ΔR with "the resolution term of the classical reliability–resolution decomposition" holds only for the covariance rearrangement, not for the Murphy–DeGroot resolution the abstract names.

Every one of these is repairable without new data or a new estimator, which is why I recommend major revision rather than rejection.

## Weaknesses

### W1: Theorem 4's printed sandwich-variance formula is wrong by a factor of G, so the theorem's consistency claim is false as written
**Problem**: Theorem 4 states that the plug-in `σ̂² = (N_*/G) Σ_g (Σ_{i∈g} ψ̂_i / N_*)² · G/(G-1)` is consistent for `σ² = lim Var(√N_*(ΔR̂ - θ))`. Simplifying, the printed expression equals `Σ_g T_g² / (N_*(G-1))`, whereas the quantity that must converge to `σ²` is `N_* · Var̂(ΔR̂) = (G/(G-1)) Σ_g T_g² / N_*`. The printed formula is `1/G` times the correct one and converges to zero, not to `σ²`. Appendix B's `Var̂(ΔR̂) = (G/(G-1)) N_*^{-2} Σ_g (Σ_{i∈g} ψ̂_i)²` is correct and is what `wager/antisymmetric.py::_cluster_interval` implements, so the theorem contradicts the appendix it cites and the code. On a synthetic dataset with `G = 400` clusters I obtain correct `σ̂² = 0.06709108`, printed-formula value `0.00016773`, ratio `0.0025 = 1/G` exactly.
**Evidence Anchor**: `equation: Theorem 4, §4.10 — the (N_*/G) prefactor in the σ̂² expression following Eq. (30)`
**Why it matters**: A false statement inside a theorem is not a typographical matter for a statistics journal: a reader implementing the interval from the theorem rather than from Appendix B would report standard errors too small by a factor of `√G` (a factor of 167 at the paper's 27,955 image clusters). The reported intervals themselves are unaffected because the code follows the appendix.
**Suggestion**: Replace the prefactor `(N_*/G)` with `N_*`, i.e. state `σ̂² = N_* · (G/(G-1)) N_*^{-2} Σ_g (Σ_{i∈g} ψ̂_i)²`, and add a one-line consistency check between the theorem and Appendix B's display so the two cannot drift again.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 5 — core expertise: cluster-robust sandwich variance estimation; verified symbolically and numerically.

### W2: Conditions (iii) and (v) of Theorem 4 cannot both bind, and the "identified-subpopulation estimand" is not well defined in the regime the paper reports
**Problem**: Condition (v) requires `φ` to take values in a fixed, finite set with strictly positive limiting mass for each level. That forces `n_c/N → π_c > 0` for every cell, hence every cell is eventually eligible, hence `N_* = N` eventually and `N_*/N → 1` almost surely. So condition (iii)'s `κ ∈ (0,1)` is unreachable given (v), and under the theorem's own conditions `θ = Σ_c π_c ΔR_c / Σ_c π_c` over identified cells is just the full-population `π`-weighted mean — the "identified-subpopulation" qualifier is vacuous inside the theorem. Conversely, in the regime actually reported (VG150: 8,614 class-pair cells, 2,268 of them singletons, cell count growing with N), the identified set is random and N-dependent, so `θ` is not a fixed parameter and the centring of `√N_*(ΔR̂ - θ)` is undefined. Appendix B says only "we treat the interval as conditional on identification", which is a reporting convention, not a definition of the limit.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §4.10 "(iii) the identified fraction N_*/N → κ ∈ (0,1] in probability"`
**Why it matters**: Task-critical: the CLT is one of the paper's four headline contributions, and as stated its estimand either collapses to the unconditional target (under (v)) or fails to exist (in the reported regime). It also means the reported identified fractions (99.0%, 100%) are decorative rather than operative: no stated theorem covers `κ < 1`.
**Suggestion**: Pick one of two honest routes. (a) Keep (v), drop (iii), define `θ = Σ_c π_c ΔR_c` over the fixed support, state that (v) implies asymptotically complete identification, and treat singleton exclusion purely as a finite-sample bias reported via coverage. (b) Replace (v) with a triangular-array condition allowing the number of cells to grow (e.g. `#{c} = o(N_*)` plus a minimum-mass condition), define `θ_N` as the conditional-on-identification target, and state the CLT for `√N_*(ΔR̂ - θ_N)` with the bias `θ_N - θ` bounded by `1 - κ_N` times the score bound. Route (b) matches the data; route (a) is the smaller edit.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 5 — core expertise: asymptotic theory for estimands defined on random subpopulations.

### W3: The proof of Theorem 4 asserts the dataset-level linearization remainder instead of deriving it, and the per-cell rate offered does not aggregate to it
**Problem**: The proof opens "The linearization above gives `ΔR̂ - θ = N_*^{-1} Σ_i ψ_i + o_p(N_*^{-1/2})`". The only linearization supplied is Appendix B's per-cell Hájek projection with an `o_p(n_c^{-1/2})` remainder, aggregated as `ΔR̂ = N_*^{-1} Σ_c n_c ΔR̂_c`. That gives an aggregate remainder `N_*^{-1} Σ_c n_c o_p(n_c^{-1/2}) = N_*^{-1} Σ_c O_p(n_c^{1/2})`, and with `C` cells of comparable size the ratio to `N_*^{-1/2}` is `√C`, which **diverges** whenever the number of cells grows. The step is therefore not justified by what precedes it; it is justified only under condition (v)'s fixed finite `C`, which the proof never invokes for this purpose (it invokes (v) only for the within-cell WLLN). The repair is available and cheap: for a bounded-kernel order-two U-statistic the *degenerate* second-order remainder is `O_p(n_c^{-1})` with independent contributions across cells, giving an aggregate `O_p(√C / N_*) = o_p(N_*^{-1/2})` whenever `C = o(N_*)` — satisfied comfortably by VG150 (`C = 6,346`, `N_* = 227,337`).
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: Appendix B, Proof of Theorem 4 "The linearization above gives" — no derivation of the aggregate remainder rate`
**Why it matters**: This is the load-bearing step of the CLT: without it the Lindeberg–Feller argument applies to the linear part but says nothing about `ΔR̂`. As written the theorem's proof has a gap precisely where the paper's Zipf-cell application is most stressed.
**Suggestion**: State the second-order (degenerate) remainder rate per cell explicitly, note that its cell contributions are independent given cell membership, and give the one-line variance calculation showing the aggregate is `o_p(N_*^{-1/2})` under `#cells = o(N_*)`. This simultaneously weakens the reliance on condition (v) and supports route (b) of W2.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 5 — core expertise: Hájek projections and remainder rates for U-statistics.

### W4: The paper's claim about its own cell-size distribution — the basis for its condition-(v) honesty statement — is false
**Problem**: The paper asserts three times that most of its eligible cells are at the minimum size: §4.10 "dominated by the minimum size n_c=2", §6.1 "most eligible VG150 cells sit at the minimum size n_c=2", Appendix B "Zipf cell sizes dominated by n_c=2". Computing the class-pair cell sizes directly from the committed `data/vg/vg_predcls.npz` test split (229,605 relations, 8,614 cells, 2,268 singletons, 6,346 eligible cells — figures that match the paper's own `results/antisymmetric_ablations.json` exactly) gives: **1,115 of 6,346 eligible cells have `n_c = 2` (17.6%)**, median eligible `n_c = 7`, mean `35.8`, and only **0.98% of identified relations** lie in `n_c = 2` cells. The claim is also refutable from the paper's own Table E.7 without any recomputation: 6,346 cells at min size 2 versus 4,060 at min size 5 means at most 2,286 cells (36%) have size 2, 3 or 4.
**Evidence Anchor**: `dataset: data/vg/vg_predcls.npz — class-pair test cell sizes: 1,115 of 6,346 eligible cells at n_c=2, median 7`
**Why it matters**: This is not incidental colour. It is the stated reason the §5.1 coverage simulation (which is built with "most identified cells hold exactly two examples") is the right finite-sample check for condition (v)'s failure, and hence the reason the reader should accept 94.0% coverage as evidence for the reported intervals. The direction happens to favour the authors — the simulation is *more* adversarial than the real data — but the argument as printed misdescribes the paper's own dataset, and the real cell-size distribution is reported nowhere in the manuscript.
**Suggestion**: Report the actual class-pair cell-size distribution (a small table or a sentence with the singleton count, the `n_c=2` count, the median and the relation-weighted mean), correct the three sentences, and re-justify the simulation as a deliberate worst case ("a design in which the small-cell stress is far heavier than in VG150") rather than as a match to the data. Note that this makes the coverage evidence stronger, not weaker.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 5 — core expertise: recomputed directly from the committed data file.

### W5: Corollary 2's worst-case bound discards the simplex constraint and is needlessly loose by a factor of 3.6, which also misdiagnoses the looseness the paper analyses at length
**Problem**: Corollary 2 bounds `Σ_y Var(b_y | c) ≤ K/4` by applying Popoviciu per label to `b_y = Pr(Y=y | φ,Z) ∈ [0,1]`. But the `b_y` lie on the simplex: `Σ_y b_y = 1`, so `Var(b_y) ≤ E[b_y²] ≤ E[b_y] = p_y(c)` and hence `Σ_y Var(b_y | c) ≤ 1 - Σ_y p_y(c)² ≤ 1 - 1/K`, i.e. `0.98` at `K = 50` rather than `12.5`. The data-free bound then becomes `|ρ| M √(K(1-1/K)) = 14.00` in place of `|ρ| K M / 2 = 50.00` — a free 3.57× tightening, with the minimum admissible `ρ̄` moving from `0.00015` to `0.00053`. I confirmed the inequality numerically over 200,000 random mixing laws (`K=6`: observed max `Σ_y Var(b_y) = 0.718` against the bound `0.833` and Popoviciu's `1.5`). Two consequences follow. First, Table 3's `50.00` row and the "Minimum ρ̄" row are both loose by construction. Second, and more importantly, the interpretive paragraph attributes the looseness to "label probabilities that in practice concentrate well away from the 0/1 extremes at which that inequality is tight" — but a large part of it is structural, not empirical: it comes from ignoring a constraint the paper already has. Relatedly, since `Σ_y p_y(1-p_y) ≤ 1 - 1/K` always, the second factor of Proposition 2's "tight, per-cell" bound is *already* essentially data-free, so the claim that the tighter form needs per-instance arrays is true only of the `H`-side factor.
**Evidence Anchor**: `equation: Eq. (20), §4.5 — the Var(b_y | c) ≤ 1/4 Popoviciu step`
**Why it matters**: Proposition 2 and Corollary 2 are contribution 2 in the intro list, they occupy a main-text table and roughly two pages of §4.5, and the paper's headline reading of that table ("more than three orders of magnitude looser than the actual bias", "an honest property of the worst-case Popoviciu bound") is partly an artefact of a discardable step. The bound remains valid and remains loose, so no empirical conclusion changes, but the corollary, the table and the diagnosis all need rewriting.
**Suggestion**: Replace `K/4` with `1 - 1/K` in Corollary 2, restate the data-free bound as `|ρ| M √(K(1-1/K))`, update Table 3's two affected rows, and rewrite the diagnostic paragraph so the residual looseness is attributed to the remaining `Σ_y Var(H_X(y)|c) ≤ K M²` step (where the `Δq_y` sum-to-zero constraint may permit a further improvement worth checking). Clarify that only the `H`-side factor of the sharper bound needs per-instance arrays.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 5 — core expertise: Cauchy–Schwarz/variance bounding; inequality checked analytically and by simulation.

### W6: ΔR is identified with the classical reliability–resolution *resolution* term, which is true only for the covariance rearrangement and false for the Murphy–DeGroot resolution
**Problem**: The abstract calls `ΔR` "the resolution term of the classical reliability–resolution decomposition of a proper score, applied to a *pair* of forecasters"; §3.1 defines classical resolution as "a covariance between the forecast and the outcome indicator within groups", citing Murphy and DeGroot–Fienberg; Corollary 3 states that at the trivial partition `ΔR̂` "is then exactly the classical resolution term ... applied without any conditioning at all, and `ΔP̂` the corresponding calibration term"; the conclusion repeats the identification. The Murphy–DeGroot resolution is `Var(E[Y | forecast])`, which equals `Cov(q, 1{Y})` only when the forecast is calibrated. I built a two-label example with two miscalibrated forecasts (`f_0 ∈ {0.05,0.95}`, `f_1 ∈ {0.35,0.65}`, true `p ∈ {0.2,0.8}`, n = 20,000): at the trivial partition WAGER returns `ΔR̂ = -0.35964444`, which matches the difference of *covariance-form* resolutions to 12 decimals (`-0.35964444`), while the difference of Murphy–DeGroot resolutions is **exactly 0** — both forecasts induce the same partition, so both have identical `Var(E[Y|q])`. Separately, "applied without any conditioning at all" is not coherent for the Murphy resolution, which is identically zero when nothing is conditioned on; and WAGER's conditioning variable is `φ`, whereas the classical decomposition conditions on the forecast itself, so the two objects have different conditioning sets even away from the trivial partition.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: Abstract "the resolution term of the classical reliability--resolution decomposition of a proper score"`
**Why it matters**: This is the paper's principal claim to statistical lineage, made in the abstract, §3.1, Corollary 3 and the conclusion. The discrepancy is largest exactly for miscalibrated pairs — which is the paper's own SGG audit (`T* = 2.50` versus `0.92`) and the reason it introduces a calibration-matching protocol. As stated, a reader who takes the abstract at face value will believe `ΔR` estimates a quantity it does not.
**Suggestion**: Name the rearrangement. Say that `ΔR` is the difference of *covariance-form* resolutions (the Yates/Bröcker rearrangement the paper already cites at §3.1), conditional on `φ` rather than on the forecast; state that this coincides with the Murphy–DeGroot resolution difference when both forecasts are calibrated within cells and give the miscalibrated counterexample or a one-line note that it does not otherwise; and adjust the abstract, §3.1, Corollary 3 and the conclusion to that wording.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 5 — core expertise: proper-score decompositions; demonstrated by explicit counterexample.

### W7: Table E.7's minimum-cell-size and granularity rows compare different estimands, and the identified fraction Appendix B promises "alongside every estimate" is not reported there
**Problem**: Appendix B states that the authors "report the identified fraction alongside every estimate". Table E.7 reports a "Cells" column but no coverage, and its rows target materially different subpopulations. Computing the identified fractions from the committed data: min cell size 2 → `N_* = 227,337` (99.0%); 5 → `221,114` (96.3%); 10 → `212,503` (92.6%); 20 → `199,089` (86.7%); and the subject-only granularity row → `229,605` (100.0%, `n_singletons = 0` in the cached JSON). The prose reads the near-constancy of `ΔR̂` across the min-size rows (`0.01439 → 0.01395 → 0.01330 → 0.01225`) as robustness of one quantity, but each row estimates the resolution gain of a different, progressively head-weighted subpopulation, so the observed drift confounds estimator sensitivity with a shifting target. Table 4 in the main text has the same problem in miniature: its `raw / quadratic / subject` row is computed at 100% coverage while the class-pair rows are at 98.9%, and neither the table nor the caption says so.
**Evidence Anchor**: `table: Table E.7 — "Cells" column, min-cell-size 20 row (1,757 cells) with no identified fraction reported`
**Why it matters**: §6.1 already identifies exactly this hazard ("cross-granularity comparisons mix the between-cell covariance term of Proposition 1 with a change of identified subpopulation"), so the paper knows the issue and does not apply its own remedy where it matters most — at 86.7% coverage, "immaterial at VG150's 99.0% coverage" no longer applies. The sensitivity table is the paper's main evidence that its conclusions do not depend on `φ` and the minimum cell size.
**Suggestion**: Add an identified-fraction (or `N_*`) column to Table E.7 and to Table 4, and re-run the minimum-cell-size and granularity rows restricted to a common identified subsample (the `n_c ≥ 20` set) so that estimator sensitivity is separated from the change of target. Report both the raw and the common-subsample versions; the difference between them is itself informative.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 5 — core expertise: estimand definition under selection; coverage figures recomputed from committed data.

## Detailed Comments

### Title & Abstract
- The title is accurate and appropriately modest ("An Exact Decomposition of Paired Score Differences"). The abstract is precise about the estimator and the identity. Two claims in it overreach: "prove asymptotic normality with a consistent cluster-robust variance" (unqualified, while §4.10 and §6.1 both state at length that the key condition is not attained in the paper's own data — a half-sentence caveat belongs in the abstract), and the resolution-term identification of W6.

### Introduction
- Contributions 1–4 are clearly and honestly stated, and §1.3 "Scope, and what is not claimed" is a model of its kind — the three interpretation limits are exactly the right three. Contribution 3 repeats the abstract's unqualified asymptotic-normality claim.
- The terminology table (Table 1) is genuinely useful and defines "coverage" against "identified fraction", which is the right vocabulary for the estimand issue.

### Literature Review / Theoretical Framework
- Outside my remit except where it bears on the estimand. §3.1's definition of classical resolution as a within-group forecast–outcome covariance is the covariance rearrangement, not Murphy–DeGroot resolution, and attributing it to Murphy and DeGroot–Fienberg without saying so is the root of W6. §3.1's point about inference not transferring from single-model variance estimators is correct and well argued.

### Methodology / Research Design

**Section 2 (worked example).** Verified number by number; see S1. Two precision points. First, the covariance paragraph does not state the divisor convention: the `3/8` is the `1/n_c` (biased) plug-in, and with the `1/(n_c-1)` convention the covariance form returns `1/2` exactly and there is no "discrepancy" to explain. Since `ΔR̂_c` *is* exactly `Σ_y` of the unbiased sample covariances (I verified this cell by cell), the cleanest version of the paragraph is: the leave-one-out estimator equals the unbiased covariance sum, the naive plug-in equals the biased one, and Proposition 3's `(n_c-1)/n_c` is precisely the ratio between the two conventions. Second, "the true frequencies (3/4,1/4)" — in a four-point example there is no population; these are the cell's empirical frequencies, and the maximality statement is about the in-sample transported gain, which is what makes it exactly (not approximately) true.

**Theorem 1.** Correct. The expansion over labels, the cancellation of the label-independent term via `Σ_y 1{Y=y} = 1`, and the corollary that a cell-constant gain vector gives `ΔR_c = 0` are all right. `ΔP_c`'s independent-coupling definition is the right one and the paper is explicit that independence, not exchangeability, is what unbiasedness needs.

**Theorem 2 / Corollary 1.** Correct and verified; see S3. The hypotheses are stronger than the proof uses (strict convexity and properness are not needed for Eq. (14)), and `ψ(q) = Σ q_k log q_k` is not differentiable on the relative boundary of `Δ_K`, so the log-score instance is formally outside the stated hypotheses at `q_k = 0` — which is exactly the case the `ε = 10⁻⁶` floor exists to handle. Also, a function defined only on `Δ_K` has no `ℝ^K` gradient; the statement needs `ψ` defined on an open neighbourhood (or on `ℝ^K_{>0}`), which both worked examples satisfy.

**Proposition 1 (coarsening).** Correct. The per-label law of total covariance is applied properly (the finer `C` determines `φ̄`, so the nesting is legitimate), and I reproduced the identity exactly on an independent explicit population in both coarse cells, including a case where the between-cell term flips the sign of the difference. The "generally not sign definite" claim and the vanishing conditions are right.

**Proposition 2 / Corollary 2 (sensitivity).** The Cauchy–Schwarz step is correct, and I appreciate that the proof openly says the first display is an identity rather than a bound. `ρ` is undefined when either variance sum is zero (the covariance is then zero too, so the bound holds trivially, but the statement should say so). The substantive problem is Corollary 2's discarded simplex constraint — see W5. The "empirical bias" in Table 3 is a difference of two aggregates computed on different identified subpopulations; see Minor issue M1.

**Theorem 3 (finite-sample identity).** Correct. The pair-counting argument is right (`H_i(y_i)` appears in `n_c-1` pairs at coefficient `1/2`; the crossed terms enumerate all ordered pairs), the identity is deterministic as claimed, and the U-statistic unbiasedness statement correctly flags independence rather than exchangeability. The `O(NK)` computation in Eq. (26) is right and matches the implementation.

**Proposition 3 (attenuation).** Correct and exact; see S2.

**Section 4.9 (inference) and the randomization test.** The Hájek contribution Eq. (28) matches `wager/antisymmetric.py` (`psi_reason = 2·pair_mean - cell_theta - reasoning`), has exactly zero sample mean, and the cluster-robust interval is the standard one-way sandwich. The cyclic-shift test is a valid exact randomization test: uniform sampling from the product of cyclic groups, the identity element is in the support, and the plus-one correction is applied — so exactness under the sharp within-cell exchangeability null is correctly claimed, and the paper correctly demotes it to a secondary relation-level diagnostic given image clustering.

**Theorem 4 (asymptotic normality).** See W1, W2, W3. Two further points. Condition (ii)'s `max_g |g| = o(√G)` is redundant given the finite third moment (the proof's Lyapunov step uses only the third moment), so the condition list is not minimal — worth trimming, since a shorter honest list is more convincing than a longer one. And the proof attributes `N_*/Σ_g Var(T_g) → 1/σ²` to condition (iii) when it is condition (iv)'s definition that supplies it. Condition (iv) — assuming the exact variance of the estimator converges — is a strong assumption that concedes much of the conclusion; deriving `σ²` from `Var(ψ_i)` and the cluster-size moments would be more informative.

**Corollary 3 (DM/GW).** The `ΔT̂` half is immediate and correct, and the observation that DM/GW target `ΔT` alone is a fair and useful placement. Two imprecisions: the corollary invokes "Theorem 4 applied to `ΔT̂`", but Theorem 4 is stated only for `ΔR̂` and its sandwich variance (the extension is easy but should be stated); and the unit test verifies the *unclustered* DM equivalence while the corollary claims the clustered form. The `ΔR̂`/`ΔP̂` half is the W6 issue.

### Results / Findings
- §5.1's controlled validation is well designed and the three discriminant-validity designs are the right three. All simulation numbers trace to `results/antisymmetric_simulation.json`.
- The 94.0%-versus-95% reading is over-interpreted: with 500 replications the binomial standard error is 1.06 percentage points, so a 1.0-point shortfall is within one standard error of nominal, and the "design expectation" it is measured against is itself a 4,000-replication Monte-Carlo estimate whose uncertainty is not reported. ### Methodological Fallacies Detected
