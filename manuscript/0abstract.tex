Benchmarks whose labels are strongly predictable from a known nuisance feature can
reward a model that fits that regularity better rather than one that predicts individual
examples better. Existing audits compare a single model against a prior-only baseline,
which does not answer the question a benchmark result actually poses: when one system
outscores another, how much of the gain came from the instances? We introduce WAGER, a
pairwise attribution algorithm. For two frozen probabilistic models it forms their
proper-score contrast, matches test examples sharing a declared prior feature, and
crosses their labels, giving an exact finite-sample identity: the observed gain equals a
prior-transported gain plus an antisymmetric instance-alignment gain. The residual is an
order-two U-statistic that, under the quadratic score, equals the within-cell
prediction--label covariance the new model has gained, connecting it to the classical
resolution term of proper-score decomposition. It runs in $O(NK)$ time, fits no baseline
model, has no tunable hyperparameter, and admits cluster-robust inference. Two further
exact results follow: leave-one-out transport removes an $(n_c-1)/n_c$ attenuation bias
that a naive in-sample plug-in incurs, and coarsening the audit cell shifts the estimate
by an explicit between-cell covariance term. On 229,605 Visual Genome relations, a
class-only network's entire improvement over the frequency baseline is prior-transported,
with exactly zero instance alignment; a model reading real pixels through a frozen CLIP
encoder scores clearly worse overall, yet aligns with individual instances significantly
better than the box-geometry model beating it, an advantage surviving both post-hoc prior
correction and calibration matching. Applied unchanged to long-tailed classification, a
calibration-matched audit overturns what the raw scores suggest two re-weighting
schedules bought.
