Two probabilistic classifiers are compared on a test set and the newer one scores higher.
When the labels depend partly on a grouping variable both models observe --- the
object-class pair in a relation-prediction benchmark, the frequency tier in a long-tailed
one --- that difference mixes two improvements: the new model may fit the label
frequencies \emph{within} groups more closely, or it may match its predictions to the
individual cases they were made for. An aggregate difference cannot say which, and
testing it for significance does not separate them. We give an exact decomposition into
those two parts. The construction is elementary: score each model's prediction for one
test case against the label of a \emph{different} case in the same group. Averaging over
such within-group relabellings isolates the gain earned from group-level label
frequencies alone; what remains is the gain from predicting individual cases. The
identity is exact in finite samples, needs no fitted baseline model, no sample splitting
and no tuning parameter, and costs $O(NK)$ for $N$ cases and $K$ labels. The remainder is
a U-statistic of order two equal to the within-group covariance between the two models'
predicted-probability difference and the label indicator --- the resolution term of the
classical reliability--resolution decomposition of a proper score, applied to a
\emph{pair} of forecasters rather than to one. We show this identity holds for every
Bregman score, prove asymptotic normality with a consistent cluster-robust variance,
bound the effect of an unrecorded grouping variable, and show that using a group's own
label frequency as the counterfactual attenuates the estimate by exactly $(n_c-1)/n_c$.
Simulations establish interval coverage and delimit what the components separate. On two
released scene-graph checkpoints, a ten-point gain in a widely reported metric is
overwhelmingly group-frequency fit.
