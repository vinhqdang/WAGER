Vision benchmarks with strong annotation priors can reward a new model even when its
gain does not come from better instance-specific visual prediction. Existing audits
compare a model with a frequency baseline, which confounds two questions: how well each
system fits the prior, and whether the \emph{difference} between systems uses information
beyond it. We introduce WAGER, \textbf{W}ithin-cell \textbf{A}ntisymmetric \textbf{G}ain
\textbf{E}valuation of \textbf{R}easoning, a pairwise attribution algorithm. For two
frozen probabilistic models, WAGER forms their proper-score contrast, matches examples
sharing a declared prior feature, and crosses their labels. This yields an exact
finite-sample identity: observed gain equals a prior-transported gain plus an
antisymmetric instance-alignment gain. The latter is an order-two U-statistic and, under
the quadratic score, equals twice the within-cell prediction--label covariance gained by
the new model, connecting it to the classical resolution term of proper-score
decomposition. It costs $O(NK)$ time, needs no fitted prior model or projection fold, and
admits cluster-robust inference and a cell-stratified randomization test. We prove two
further exact results: leave-one-out transport removes a finite-sample attenuation bias
of $(n_c-1)/n_c$ that a naive in-sample plug-in prior incurs, and coarsening the audit
cell shifts the estimate by an explicit between-cell covariance term. On 229,605 Visual
Genome relations, a class-only network improves the frequency baseline entirely through
the prior channel, whereas box geometry adds an alignment gain of $0.01439$
(95\% CI $[0.01373,0.01504]$). Applied unchanged to long-tailed classification, WAGER
separates two re-weighting schedules that aggregate scores cannot: one whose near-zero
total gain ($+0.00143$) conceals a $+0.19713$ prior gain cancelling a $-0.19569$
alignment loss, and one whose genuine $+0.06606$ improvement is two-thirds
prior-recoverable and one-third instance alignment, concentrated on rare classes. WAGER
therefore attributes a model-to-model improvement directly, without treating a separately
estimated baseline as ground truth.
