\subsection{The disease}
On the most label-imbalanced vision benchmarks, a trivial predictor that looks up the
most frequent label given some cheap context already recovers most of the headline
metric:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scene Graph Generation (VG150).} The FREQ baseline of Zellers et
    al.~\cite{zellers2018neural}---predict
    $\arg\max_{p} P(\text{predicate} \mid \text{subject}, \text{object})$ from
    training counts---reaches $R@50 \approx 60.6$ on PredCls versus MOTIFS at
    $65.2$. A lookup table recovers $\sim\!96\%$ of ``state-of-the-art'' recall.
    \item \textbf{Visual Question Answering.} Answering ``yes'' blindly to ``Do you
    see a\dots'' reaches $\sim\!87\%$ on the original unbalanced VQA
    \cite{goyal2017making}. On the changing-prior split VQA-CP
    \cite{agrawal2018dont}, UpDn collapses from $\sim\!63\%$ to $\sim\!40\%$.
    Recent vision-language models remain measurably reliant on such language priors
    \cite{lee2025vlind, wu2025lanp}.
    \item \textbf{Long-tail detection (LVIS).} Average precision on rare categories is
    dominated by image-frequency priors \cite{gupta2019lvis}.
\end{itemize}
Figure~\ref{fig:concept} illustrates the mechanism on a real annotation, and
Section~\ref{sec:exp} (Figure~\ref{fig:longtail}) shows that on Visual~Genome the five
most frequent predicates already account for $72\%$ of all relation instances.

\subsection{Why existing fixes are not enough}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{mean Recall@K} \cite{chen2019knowledge, tang2019learning} rebalances
    metric weight but can be gamed by suppressing head predictions, and it breaks
    category independence \cite{li2022rethinking}.
    \item \textbf{VQA-CP OOD splits} can be solved by \emph{inverting} known label
    statistics; a random-answer method can beat the state of the art on some question
    types---a textbook Goodhart failure \cite{teney2020value, shrestha2020negative}.
    \item \textbf{Causal TDE} \cite{tang2020unbiased} and modern debiasing methods
    \cite{gao2025informative} are training- or inference-time interventions, not
    \emph{evaluations} that quantify how much of a reported gain is confound.
\end{itemize}
Every one of these is bespoke to one benchmark and gives a point estimate with no
uncertainty quantification. None answers ``is \emph{this specific gain} real, and with
what confidence?''

\subsection{The gap WAGER fills}
We present a general, statistically principled, cross-benchmark test that decomposes a
reported gain into a prior-recoverable part and a residual reasoning part, with a
finite-sample validity guarantee. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The self-prior projection} (Definition~\ref{def:proj}): a new
    estimator that compares a model to its own frequency-collapsed self, making the
    null exact without knowing the true prior and the measurement provably
    calibration-invariant (Corollary~\ref{cor:calib}).
    \item \textbf{One process, two readouts}
    (Theorems~\ref{thm:evalue}--\ref{thm:growth}): a single testing-by-betting capital
    process both \emph{certifies} that a model reasons beyond the prior (an
    anytime-valid e-value) and \emph{quantifies} how much, via its growth rate.
    \item \textbf{The Reasoning Gain Ratio} (Definition~\ref{def:rgr},
    Theorem~\ref{thm:rgr}): a gain-attribution statistic with a distribution-free,
    anytime-valid confidence interval and an operational decision rule.
    \item \textbf{Validation}: a simulation suite establishing type-I control, nominal
    coverage, and faithful recovery of injected reasoning, followed by a real
    Visual~Genome study over $7.6{\times}10^5$ relations in which the framework
    certifies the frequency baseline as non-reasoning and flags every accuracy gain
    over it as non-substantive.
\end{enumerate}
The framework operates entirely on cached probability vectors, so a benchmark is
analyzed in minutes on a CPU once logits have been dumped.
