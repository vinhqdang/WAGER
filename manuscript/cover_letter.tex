Dear Editors,

I am submitting the manuscript ``WAGER: Within-cell Antisymmetric Gain Evaluation of
Resolution'' for consideration as a regular paper in \emph{Array}.

The paper addresses a measurement problem that recurs across machine learning: when a
benchmark's labels are strongly predictable from a known nuisance feature, a new model
can raise its headline score by fitting that regularity more precisely rather than by
predicting individual examples better. Scene graph generation, visual question
answering and long-tailed recognition all have this structure. The usual response is to
compare a model against a prior-only baseline, but that does not answer the question a
benchmark result actually poses: when system B outscores system A, how much of the gain
came from the instances and how much from the prior?

WAGER answers that question directly, and the contribution is primarily methodological.
Given two frozen probabilistic models, it forms their proper-score contrast, matches
test examples sharing a declared prior feature, and crosses their labels. This yields an
exact finite-sample identity --- the observed gain equals a prior-transported gain plus
an antisymmetric instance-alignment gain --- whose residual is an order-two U-statistic
that, under the quadratic score, equals the within-cell prediction--label covariance the
new model has gained, linking it to the classical resolution term of proper-score
decomposition. The estimator runs in $O(NK)$ time on cached predictions, fits no
baseline model, has no tunable hyperparameter, and comes with cluster-robust intervals
and a cell-stratified randomization test. Two further exact results are proved:
leave-one-out transport removes an attenuation bias of $(n_c-1)/n_c$ that a naive
in-sample plug-in incurs, and coarsening the audit cell shifts the estimate by an
explicit between-cell covariance term.

Because the method is a measuring instrument, the paper takes its validation seriously.
Beyond recovery and null designs, we verify the coverage of the primary interval in a
regime that stresses it, and we report discriminant-validity experiments establishing
what the two channels do and do not separate --- including an honest limitation, that a
monotone recalibration moves score mass between the channels, together with the
calibration-matched protocol that removes it and a demonstration that the protocol
works.

The empirical results show the instrument earning its keep. On 229,605 Visual Genome
relations, a class-only network's entire improvement over the frequency baseline is
provably prior-transported, with exactly zero instance alignment. More strikingly, when
a frozen CLIP encoder replaces annotation-derived box geometry, the resulting model
scores $0.04655$ \emph{lower} overall --- an evaluator reading that number would discard
it --- yet aligns with individual instances significantly \emph{better} than the model
that beats it ($+0.00641$, $p\le.002$), its entire deficit lying in the annotation
prior; the advantage survives both post-hoc prior correction and calibration matching.
Applied unchanged to long-tailed image classification, the same estimator separates two
re-weighting schedules whose aggregate scores hide their composition, and a
calibration-matched reanalysis overturns what the raw decomposition appears to say about
each of them.

I believe the work suits \emph{Array}'s broad computer-science scope: it is a
methodological and measurement contribution, with proofs, a reference implementation,

scripts needed to reproduce every number and figure are publicly available, and the
repository URL is given on the title page.

The manuscript is original, has not been published previously, and is not under

I declare no competing financial or personal interests, and the work received no specific

supplied on the separate title page, with the manuscript itself anonymized for the
