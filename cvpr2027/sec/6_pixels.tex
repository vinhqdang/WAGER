\section{The model every metric ranks last}\label{sec:vgvisual}

The predictors of \cref{sec:data} never see an image: they read the class pair and, at most,
box coordinates from the annotation. On the same reconstructed split we add one that reads pixels --- subject and object
regions cropped from the image and encoded by a frozen CLIP ViT-B/32~\cite{radford2021learning},
concatenated with the class embeddings MLP-CLASS uses. CLIP is not fine-tuned, so any
case-level gain reflects what pretraining had already recovered. All three compared models
are retrained on one fixed subsample of $100{,}000$ training relations, so differences isolate
the features rather than the amount of data.

By every aggregate measure the CLIP model is the worst of the three: top-1 accuracy $0.6156$
against $0.6467$ for the geometry model, and a quadratic score $0.04642$ lower. An evaluator
reading either number would discard it. The split disagrees. With each model's confidence matched on
held-out images, the CLIP model loses $0.04705$ of group-level score against the geometry
model while \emph{gaining} $0.00464$ of case-level score (CI $[+0.00311,+0.00618]$); on raw
outputs the figures are $0.05282$ and $0.00640$ (CI $[0.00513,0.00767]$, $p=.002$). Its whole
deficit is group-level (\cref{fig:where}). Against the class-only model, pixels buy $0.01663$
of case-level score on raw outputs where box geometry buys $0.01023$, with well-separated
intervals.

A larger case-level part is not the same as better ranking, and here the two disagree. With
each model's prior matched within cells, the CLIP model still ranks the correct predicate
first less often than the geometry model: top-1 accuracy $2.17$ points lower, MRR $1.67$
lower, recall@5 $1.07$ lower. Its probability moves towards the correct predicate within a
pair more than the geometry model's does, but not by enough, often enough, to overtake it.
So the CLIP model carries more case-level covariance without being the better ranker. The
within-cell AUC, which no calibration or prior shift can move, does not settle it either way:
$0.5408$ against $0.5323$, a difference of $+0.0085$ with interval $[-0.0062,+0.0214]$, while
both models clear the class-only model's $0.5$ by $+0.0408$ and $+0.0323$. The CLIP model's
case-level advantage is real in the covariance the split measures; that it is better
recognition is not established.

\paragraph{Seeds.} Over five training seeds (seed $0$ reproduces the predictions above
exactly; \cref{app:vgseeds}), the CLIP model's matched case-level advantage over geometry stays
between $+0.00413$ and $+0.00474$, every interval excluding zero, its accuracy stays below
geometry's, and its AUC difference is positive in all five but excludes zero in one. Adding
the CLIP features to geometry, rather than replacing it, doubles that advantage ($+0.00807$ to
$+0.00949$).

Two checks support this reading. Post-hoc correction of a model's label prior should move
only the group-level part, and it does: $+0.02390$ group-level against $+0.00114$ case-level.
Applied to the CLIP model it halves the aggregate deficit, from $-0.04642$ to $-0.02138$,
while the case-level advantage persists ($+0.00754$; $+0.00664$ with both models corrected).

\paragraph{Beyond scene graphs.}\label{sec:further} Nothing in \cref{sec:method} is specific
to relations, and the supplementary applies the identical estimator to long-tailed image
classification on CIFAR-100-LT across seeds and imbalance ratios, with superclasses as the
grouping, and to long-tailed text classification. On CIFAR-100-LT two standard re-weighting
schedules turn out to have opposite compositions once confidence is matched, reversing what
their raw scores suggest about both. The constraint on the grouping bites here: the fine
class label cannot serve as a cell, because it leaves every cell label-homogeneous and
forces the case-level part to zero by construction --- an artefact that reads exactly like a
null result.
