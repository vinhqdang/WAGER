\begin{abstract}
Debiasing methods for scene graph generation are judged by mean recall, and one of the best
known, Total Direct Effect (TDE), raises it on Visual Genome from 14.6 to 24.8. What did
those ten points buy? A model raises mean recall either by predicting rare predicates more
often within each subject--object pair, or by recognising which individual images actually
show them, and the metric credits both alike. We give an exact way to tell them apart.
Scoring each prediction against the label of a different relation from the same
subject--object group keeps the group's predicate frequencies but breaks the link between
each relation and its own label; this splits the difference between two frozen models, in
a proper score or in mean recall itself, into a group-level part and a case-level
covariance, with no fitted baseline, in $O(NK)$ time, with image-clustered intervals. On
TDE's released checkpoint, 87\% of the mean-recall gain is group-level and almost none of it
comes from the fifteen rarest predicates. A one-line logit adjustment of the baseline, which
leaves the ranking of relations within every group unchanged, recovers nine of the ten points
with the same split, at under a tenth of TDE's cost in recall, and a threshold-free
within-group AUC finds no discrimination that TDE gained. A second released method, IETrans,
gains 21 points, 97\% group-level, while its within-group discrimination falls under both
proper scores. The converse also occurs: a CLIP
model reading image crops, ranked last on accuracy and on the proper score, discriminates
better among relations sharing an object pair than the geometry model ranked above it.
\end{abstract}
